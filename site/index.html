    Recursive Continuance Tour Bus

    A guided ride through the numbers that describe themselves — from Euclid's leftovers to the most irrational number alive.

    Route: Depot → Golden Milestone → Cattle Crossing → The Casino → Hall of Mirrors → Terminus — 15 stops, no transfers.
    Stop 01

    The Depot

    Where every route begins: Euclid's algorithm.

    Every tour starts where Euclid did. To find gcd(a, b) you divide, keep the remainder, and repeat — and the sequence of quotients you were about to throw away is, quietly, the continued fraction of a/b. Nothing is wasted on this route.

    Type a number or a fraction into the machine below and step through it. The quotients pile up as CF terms; the convergents are the running best guesses.

    Stop 02

    The Unfolding Road

    The same algorithm, run on an irrational, never arrives.

    Run divide-and-flip on an irrational and it simply never terminates. We record the result as [a0; a1, a2, …] — the semicolon marks the whole-number part, the commas the reciprocal descent. A number is rational exactly when the road ends.

    The machine above handles both: a finite ledger for p/q, an endless one (certified term-by-term) for π and e.

    Next stop
    Stop 03

    The Engine Room

    Convergents, and why they are the best deal in town.

    Convergents are the CF's pistons: pn = anpn-1 + pn-2, with the same recurrence driving qn. Each convergent is the best rational approximation for its size, with error below 1/qn². That is how π coughs up 22/7, then the uncanny 355/113.

    Next stop
    Stop 04

    The Golden Milestone

    All ones — the slowest climb on the whole line.

    φ = [1; 1, 1, 1, …] is the slowest-converging continued fraction there is: all ones means each step gains as little as possible. Hurwitz's theorem makes this precise and crowns φ "the most irrational number." The golden spiral is what that stubbornness looks like.

    Next stop
    Stop 05

    Scenic Overlook

    Best approximation, applied to the calendar.

    Stop for the view: convergents are the cheapest rationals that hit a target. Calendars are the case study — 8 leap days every 33 years (Khayyam, 1079) is a convergent of the tropical year; the Gregorian 97/400 is close but, tellingly, not one.

    Next stop
    Stop 06

    The Loop Road

    Periodic continued fractions and the quadratic irrationals.

    Here the road bends back on itself. A CF is eventually periodic exactly when its value is a quadratic irrational (Lagrange), so √d always has a repeating block: √2 = [1; 2, 2, 2, …] = [1; (2)].

    Next stop
    Stop 07

    The Cattle Crossing

    Pell's equation, straight out of the convergents of √d.

    Pell's equation x² − d·y² = 1 falls out of the convergents of √d. Fermat's 1657 challenge, d = 61, has a smallest solution so large it stampedes: x = 1 766 319 049. Set the dial and watch a convergent land exactly on the fence.

    Next stop
    Stop 08

    The Family Tree

    Every positive rational, exactly once.

    The Stern–Brocot tree lists every positive rational exactly once, each fraction the mediant (a+c)/(b+d) of its neighbours. Turn left or right and you are reading off a continued fraction in disguise.

    Next stop
    Stop 09

    Celebrity Sightings

    e keeps a pattern; π keeps a secret.

    Not all irrationals behave alike. e = [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, …] hides a crisp arithmetic pattern; π's expansion marches on with no known rule at all — 3, 7, 15, 1, 292, …. Load each into the machine and look.

    Next stop
    Stop 10

    The Casino

    The Gauss map, and the constant almost every number obeys.

    The Gauss map T(x) = {1/x} shuffles the CF deck, dealing off one term per turn. Its statistics are iron: for almost every real, the geometric mean of the partial quotients tends to Khinchin's constant K₀ ≈ 2.6854520 — no matter which number you sat down with.

    Next stop
    Stop 11

    The Infinite Assembly Line

    Gosper's streaming arithmetic on continued fractions.

    Gosper's insight: you can do arithmetic on continued fractions term-by-term, as a stream, never fully evaluating anything. Feed in √2 and √3 and the line emits the CF of their sum, one certified quotient at a time.

    Next stop
    Stop 12

    The Tower

    Recursion that climbs out of sight.

    Recursion can climb terrifyingly fast. Ackermann's A(4,2) = 2^65536 − 3 — a number we can print the size of but never the whole of. The Y combinator, at the other extreme, achieves recursion with no name to call at all: self-reference distilled.

    Stop 13

    The Hall of Mirrors

    Fractals: shapes that quote themselves.

    A fractal is a shape that quotes itself. Koch's snowflake has dimension ln4/ln3 ≈ 1.262; Sierpiński's gasket ln3/ln2 ≈ 1.585; the dragon and Hilbert curves pack the plane so densely their dimension reaches 2.

    Stop 14

    The Souvenir Shop

    Music, broken RSA, and an unruly sequence.

    Souvenirs from the whole route. Equal temperament is the convergent 7/12 of log₂(3/2); Wiener's attack breaks RSA when the secret exponent is too small, reading it straight off a continued fraction; and Collatz simply refuses to be tamed.

    Next stop
    Stop 15

    Terminus

    End of the line — the questions nobody can answer yet.

    End of the line. Is the geometric mean of π's partial quotients really Khinchin's constant? Is K₀ itself irrational? Does every Collatz orbit reach 1? Nobody knows.

    Open track ahead

    Three unanswered questions that this very tour walked right past:

    • Khinchin for π. Numerically, π's partial quotients look like a "typical" real (geometric mean ≈ K₀), but there is no proof π is not exceptional.
    • Is K₀ irrational? Khinchin's constant is not known to be irrational, let alone transcendental.
    • Collatz. Verified past 268 by computer; still unproved for all n.

    Encore

    Colliding Blocks Count π

    Where recursion, rotation, continued fractions, and π turn out to be one idea.

    One last stop, off the main line. Put a small block between a wall and a big one, send the big block in, and count every perfectly elastic collision. Galperin's theorem: with mass ratio 100ⁿ, the number of collisions is the first n+1 digits of π. The dynamics is secretly a billiard in a wedge — the collision count is a rotation number, exactly what continued fractions were built to measure, and π is the half-turn.

    The whole tour closes its loop here — recursion, continued fractions, rotation numbers, and π, all one idea. Run it in the terminal with python -m tourbus demo blocks.

    Cross-Domain

    The Hofstadter Butterfly

    The same recurrence, now in a magnetic field: number theory drawn onto a material.

    Put an electron on a crystal lattice in a magnetic field. Its allowed energies, plotted against the magnetic flux p/q through each cell, form this self-similar fractal — the Hofstadter butterfly. To compute the spectrum you count eigenvalues with the Sturm sequence dk = (ak−E)dk−1 − dk−2 — which is the continuant, the very recurrence behind every continued-fraction convergent. The butterfly's gaps are labelled by the continued-fraction convergents of the flux; at irrational flux the spectrum is a Cantor set.

    Every wing is computed live by the continuant. The whole Cross-Domain Line — chemistry, condensed matter, engineering, dynamics, biology — is one python -m tourbus crossdomain away.
